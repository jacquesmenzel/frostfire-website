How Often Should You Change Your HVAC Air Filter?

A clear filter schedule for North Carolina homes — including pets, allergies, MERV ratings, and the mistakes that freeze AC coils.

Updated 2026-07-10 · Written by Frost Fire Heating & Cooling · Serving Clayton, Raleigh, Durham & the Triangle

If you do only one DIY HVAC task, make it this: change the air filter on schedule. In the Triangle, pollen, humidity, and long cooling seasons clog filters faster than generic "every 90 days" advice suggests.

This guide covers how often to change your HVAC air filter, which MERV rating makes sense, and how the wrong filter can cause frozen coils and expensive AC repairs.

Why Filters Matter More Than People Think

Filters protect the blower and coil from dust. When clogged, airflow drops, the evaporator coil gets too cold, ice forms, and cooling dies — often with water damage when it thaws.

Filters also affect indoor air quality. They are not a substitute for source control or proper ventilation, but a loaded filter is both an airflow problem and an IAQ problem.

How Often to Change Filters in the Triangle

Use this as a starting schedule, then adjust by inspecting monthly:

  • 1 inch standard filter, no pets: every 30–60 days in cooling season; 60–90 days lighter seasons
  • Pets (especially multiple): every 30 days, sometimes sooner
  • Allergies / NC pollen peaks: every 30 days in spring and fall
  • Construction / renovations nearby: every 2–4 weeks until dust settles
  • Thicker media cabinets (4–5 inch): often 3–6 months, but still inspect — do not assume

Summer rule If the AC runs all afternoon in Clayton or Raleigh heat, check the filter monthly even if the box says 90 days.

MERV Ratings Without the Marketing Fog

MERV measures how small a particle a filter can capture. Higher is not always better for every system. Too-restrictive filters on systems not designed for them raise static pressure and cut airflow.

Many residential systems do well with MERV 8–11. MERV 13 can be appropriate on systems designed for it or with media cabinets. If a high-MERV filter caused weak airflow, step down and ask a tech to measure static pressure.

Size, Fit, and Airflow Direction

Use the exact size printed on the old filter (for example 16x25x1). "Close enough" sizes bypass dirty air around the edges.

Install with the arrow pointing toward the furnace/air handler (the direction of airflow). A backwards filter still filters somewhat but is not designed to seal and load correctly.

Common Filter Mistakes We See

  1. Running months past a clogged filter because "it didn't look that bad"
  2. Buying the cheapest high-MERV filter that chokes an older system
  3. Forgetting a second filter location (some homes have return grilles and a furnace slot)
  4. Leaving plastic shipping wrap on a new filter (yes, it happens)
  5. Never checking washable filters — they still need cleaning

Filters vs. Real Indoor Air Quality Solutions

A filter helps, but IAQ also depends on humidity control, ventilation, and source removal. If someone in the home has asthma or severe allergies, ask about indoor air quality options beyond a grocery-store filter.

Pair Filter Changes With Professional Maintenance

Filters are DIY. Coil cleaning, capacitor testing, and drain clearing are not. Combine monthly filter discipline with twice-yearly professional maintenance for the best results in North Carolina.

When Changing the Filter Does Not Fix Cooling

If a new filter does not restore cooling, move to the broader checklist in our AC not working guide, then call for service. Capacitors, refrigerant leaks, and failed motors will not care how new your filter is.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can a dirty filter freeze my AC?

Yes. Restricted airflow is a leading cause of evaporator icing.

Are expensive filters always better?

Not always. The best filter is one that fits, is changed on time, and matches what your system can push air through.

Do heat pumps use the same filters?

Yes for shared air handlers. Change on the same schedule.

Should I run the fan constantly with a high-MERV filter?

Only if comfort/IAQ goals call for it and the system can handle the static pressure. Ask a tech if unsure.

Where is my filter located?

Common places: return grille on a wall/ceiling, or a slot at the furnace/air handler. Some homes have both.
